01

An Image is Worth 16x16 Words: Transformers for Image Recognition at Scale (ViT)

Dosovitskiy et al. (Google Research, Brain Team), 2020 2020 · arXiv:2010.11929

The problem

For a decade, computer vision was synonymous with convolutional neural networks. CNNs bake in strong inductive biases — locality, translation equivariance, a hierarchical receptive field. Transformers had already swept NLP, but applying full self-attention to every pixel is quadratic and intractable for real images. Could a near-vanilla Transformer compete in vision at all?

The contribution

Split an image into a grid of fixed-size patches (e.g. 16×16 pixels), linearly project each patch into an embedding, prepend a learnable [class] token, add 1-D position embeddings, and feed the resulting token sequence into a standard Transformer encoder. No convolutions in the backbone. The key finding: with weak inductive bias, ViT underperforms CNNs on small datasets, but when pretrained on large corpora (ImageNet-21k, JFT-300M) it matches or beats the best CNNs while costing less to train.

Why it matters to a practising engineer

ViT is the vision backbone underneath almost everything later in this deck: CLIP, Flamingo's resampler, and LLaVA's projector all consume ViT (or CLIP-ViT) patch features. Understanding that an image is "just a short token sequence" explains why modern VLMs can interleave image and text tokens in a single context window — and why image token count (patches) directly drives context length and cost.

The data-scale lesson

ViT's headline result is really a scaling claim: removing inductive bias is only viable if you replace it with data. On ImageNet-1k alone ViT loses to ResNets; pretrained on JFT-300M it wins. This "bias vs data" trade-off recurs everywhere in multimodal work — the bitter lesson, applied to pixels.

Learning Transferable Visual Models From Natural Language Supervision (CLIP)

Radford et al. (OpenAI), 2021 2021 · arXiv:2103.00020

The problem

Standard vision models are trained to predict a fixed set of labels (the 1000 ImageNet classes). Adapting to a new task means collecting new labelled data and retraining a classification head. Meanwhile the web contains hundreds of millions of images already paired with descriptive text — an enormous, free, open-vocabulary supervision signal that was going unused.

The contribution

Contrastive Language–Image Pretraining. Train an image encoder and a text encoder jointly on ~400M image–text pairs. For each batch, compute the cosine similarity matrix between all images and all captions; the symmetric InfoNCE loss pulls matching pairs together and pushes mismatched pairs apart. The result is a shared embedding space. At inference, classify zero-shot by embedding the candidate label prompts (e.g. "a photo of a {dog}") and taking the nearest caption — no task-specific training.

Why it matters to a practising engineer

CLIP is the most reused multimodal component in production. Its image encoder is the default vision tower for LLaVA and many open VLMs; its embedding space powers zero-shot classification, image search, deduplication, content moderation, and diffusion-model conditioning. When someone says "embed an image", they usually mean a CLIP-family encoder. Knowing the prompt-engineering quirk ("a photo of a ..." templates meaningfully change accuracy) is directly actionable.

Open-vocabulary, with caveats

CLIP's zero-shot generality comes from scale and natural-language labels, but it inherits web biases and struggles with fine-grained counting, OCR, and abstract reasoning. It is an alignment model, not a generative one — it scores image–text agreement; it does not produce captions. That generative gap is exactly what Flamingo and LLaVA fill.

Flamingo: a Visual Language Model for Few-Shot Learning

Alayrac et al. (DeepMind), 2022 2022 · arXiv:2204.14198

The problem

Large language models are expensive to train and exhibit powerful few-shot, in-context abilities. Vision encoders like CLIP are also expensive. Retraining a giant multimodal model end-to-end is wasteful — and risks destroying the LM's language competence. How do you give an existing, frozen LLM the ability to see, and to do few-shot multimodal tasks from interleaved image-and-text prompts?

The contribution

Flamingo keeps both the vision encoder and the LM frozen and bridges them with two new trainable components: a Perceiver Resampler that maps a variable number of vision features into a fixed small set of visual tokens, and gated cross-attention dense layers interleaved into the frozen LM. The tanh gating starts at zero, so training begins as the original LM and gradually learns to attend to visual tokens — preserving language ability. It handles arbitrarily interleaved image/text sequences and sets few-shot SOTA across captioning and VQA.

Only the resampler and the gated cross-attention layers are trained; the dim boxes above are frozen. This is the canonical "adapter" pattern for multimodality — cheap to train, modality-extensible, and non-destructive to the base model.

Why it matters to a practising engineer

Flamingo established the dominant blueprint: freeze the heavy components, train a small bridge. The Perceiver Resampler answers a real engineering question — how to feed a fixed, affordable number of visual tokens into a context window regardless of image resolution. Gated cross-attention is the template for adding any new modality (audio, video) to a deployed LLM without full retraining. The open-source OpenFlamingo and IDEFICS reproductions make the pattern directly usable.

Why zero-init gating matters

Initialising the cross-attention gate at tanh(0)=0 means the augmented model is identical to the frozen LM at step 0. Training only ever adds capability, never regresses base language quality. This zero-init-residual trick reappears in LoRA, ControlNet, and most modern adapter methods — learn it once, recognise it everywhere.

Visual Instruction Tuning (LLaVA)

Liu et al. (University of Wisconsin–Madison, Microsoft Research, Columbia), 2023 2023 · arXiv:2304.08485

The problem

Instruction tuning made text LLMs into helpful assistants, but no comparable multimodal instruction-following dataset existed. Flamingo-style models were powerful yet closed and expensive. Could you build an open, conversational vision assistant cheaply — without DeepMind-scale resources?

The contribution

Two ideas. First, use GPT-4 (text-only) to generate multimodal instruction data: feed it image captions and bounding boxes as a textual proxy for the image, and have it produce conversations, detailed descriptions, and complex-reasoning Q&A. Second, a minimal architecture: take a frozen CLIP ViT-L/14 vision encoder, connect it to a Vicuna LLM through a single linear projection (later an MLP), and instruction-tune. The whole thing trains in hours on a handful of GPUs, yet yields a capable chat-style visual assistant.

The projection W is the entire bridge: it maps CLIP image features into the LLM's word-embedding space so the language model treats them like ordinary tokens. Stage 1 trains only W (feature alignment); stage 2 fine-tunes W + the LLM on instruction data.

Why it matters to a practising engineer

LLaVA is the practical recipe the open community actually builds on. It proves you can stand up a competent VLM with a frozen CLIP tower, a tiny projector, and good data — on a startup budget. The "use a strong LLM to synthesise instruction data" trick is a reusable pattern far beyond vision. LLaVA-1.5 / NeXT, and most open multimodal models since, are direct descendants of this projector-plus-instruction-tuning template.

Data quality over architecture

LLaVA's lesson is that the bridge can be trivial (one matrix) if the data is good. Most of its capability comes from GPT-4-generated instruction data, not architectural cleverness. When building your own VLM, spend your effort on the instruction dataset — reasoning, descriptions, multi-turn dialogue — before reaching for a fancier connector.

Robust Speech Recognition via Large-Scale Weak Supervision (Whisper)

Radford et al. (OpenAI), 2022 2022 · arXiv:2212.04356

The problem

State-of-the-art ASR relied on either small high-quality supervised datasets (limited robustness) or unsupervised pretraining that still needed fine-tuning per dataset and was brittle out-of-distribution. The field lacked a single model that "just worked" across accents, noise, languages, and domains without per-task adaptation.

The contribution

Scale up weak supervision: collect 680,000 hours of multilingual, multitask audio paired with transcripts scraped from the web (with filtering to remove machine-generated transcripts). Train a single off-the-shelf encoder–decoder Transformer on log-Mel spectrogram inputs. A set of special tokens turns it into a multitask interface: the same model does transcription, X→English translation, language identification, and timestamp prediction, with strong zero-shot robustness that approaches human accuracy on many benchmarks — no dataset-specific fine-tuning.

Special tokens (<|transcribe|>, <|translate|>, language tags, <|notimestamps|>) prefix the decoder to select the task — a clean example of the prompt-as-control idea applied to a non-text modality.

Why it matters to a practising engineer

Whisper is the default open speech model. It is the audio front-end of countless agent and voice pipelines: speech → Whisper → LLM → TTS. It runs locally (multiple sizes, tiny to large-v3), is permissively licensed, and the faster-whisper / whisper.cpp ports make real-time transcription cheap. Knowing its 30-second chunking, language-ID behaviour, and hallucination-on-silence failure mode is essential for shipping voice features.

The weak-supervision thesis

Whisper's core claim mirrors ViT and CLIP: scale of noisy, diverse data beats clean, narrow data for robustness. It deliberately trades a little in-distribution accuracy for huge gains in zero-shot generalisation — the right bet for production systems that face messy real-world audio rather than a curated test set.

06

What to Take Away

  • Vision became sequence modelling. ViT recast an image as a short token sequence, letting the entire Transformer toolbox transfer to pixels — the precondition for everything else.
  • Alignment is the reusable primitive. CLIP's shared image–text embedding space is the most-reused multimodal component: search, zero-shot classification, diffusion conditioning, and the vision tower for VLMs.
  • Freeze the giants, train a small bridge. Flamingo's frozen-backbone + gated cross-attention pattern (with zero-init gating) is the cheap, non-destructive blueprint for adding modalities.
  • Data beats connector complexity. LLaVA proved a one-matrix projector plus GPT-4-synthesised instruction data yields a strong open assistant on a tiny budget.
  • Weak supervision at scale buys robustness. Whisper turned 680k hours of noisy web audio into a single multitask model that is now the default speech front-end for voice agents.
  • One recurring thesis: across ViT, CLIP, and Whisper, removing inductive bias and scaling diverse data wins — provided you actually have the data.
